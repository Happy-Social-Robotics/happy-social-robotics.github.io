Unit 7 of 8

Methods and ethics

A reviewer will check your question, your design, your measures and your treatment of participants, in about that order. This unit covers each, using the standard references the field expects you to know.

1. From topic to question

Narrow in steps. The example below is illustrative, not from a paper.

  1. Field: social robots in education.
  2. Population and setting: preschoolers listening to a story at school.
  3. Behavior: the robot's gaze toward the book versus toward the child.
  4. Outcome: how much of the story the child recalls afterward.
  5. Question: does gaze toward the book increase recall compared with gaze toward the child?

A question in this form has an independent variable, a dependent variable and a comparison. Unit 5 and Unit 6 supply the theory that says why you expect a difference.

2. Kinds of study

DesignGood forWatch out for
Lab experimentCausal claims about one variableArtificial setting; small samples
Online video studyMany participants, fast, cheapWatching a robot differs from meeting one
Field or deployment studyReal use, real constraintsMessy data; things fail. The 2026 workshop "Failing Forward" collects deployment lessons.
Interview or co-designNeeds, values, early ideasNot generalizable; careful consent
Long-term studyNovelty wearing off, relationshipsCost, dropout, a robot that must keep working

Between-subjects designs give each participant one condition and avoid carry-over effects. Within-subjects designs use each participant in every condition, which needs fewer people but needs counterbalancing of order.

3. Wizard of Oz

Claim
Many studies have a hidden person operate the robot's perception or speech. It is a legitimate technique if reported properly.
Evidence
Riek (2012) reviewed 54 Wizard of Oz experiments from 2001 to 2011. Most used it for verbal and nonverbal processing, but only about 5% reported training the wizard and under 4% measured wizard error. She proposed reporting guidelines organized around four components: robot, user, wizard and general.
Implication
If you use a wizard, say who it was, how they were trained, what choices they had, and whether participants believed the robot was autonomous.
Open question
How much do wizard errors, which are often invisible, change results?

4. Measures

Questionnaires

  • Godspeed (Bartneck et al., 2009): five scales, namely anthropomorphism, animacy, likeability, perceived intelligence and perceived safety. The authors note an overlap between anthropomorphism and animacy, and recommend checking reliability (Cronbach's alpha) and correlations. They suggest adding dummy items to mask the purpose of the questionnaire.
  • RoSAS (Carpinella et al., 2017): 18 items, developed across four studies drawing on Godspeed and social perception research. It measures warmth, competence and discomfort.

Behavioral and other measures

Task performance, recall, time, distance kept, gaze to the robot and conversation length are all used. Bartneck et al. also mention physiological measures such as skin conductivity, and note practical difficulties in scoring them.

Reliability and sample size

A measure with several items per concept lets you check whether participants answered consistently. Bartneck et al. recommend more participants, guided by a power analysis, when an expected difference is small. This matters: the education review in Unit 4 reports an average of roughly 17 to 24 participants per data point.

5. Ethics

Before you recruit anyone, get approval from your institution's ethics review board, and ask your supervisor which one applies to you. Obtain informed consent. For children, consent comes from parents or guardians and the child's assent should be sought as well. Decide what you record, where you store it and who can see it.

Claim
Robots in care raise specific ethical concerns that go beyond general research ethics.
Evidence
Sharkey and Sharkey (2012) name six concerns about robot care for older people: reduced human contact; objectification and loss of control; loss of privacy; restriction of personal liberty; deception and infantilization; and responsibility when things go wrong. They conclude that the worry is about how robots are used, not about using them at all, and call for guidelines.
Implication
Include a short section in your paper on how your study respects these points. The PARO question from Unit 1 belongs here: is it deceptive for a person with dementia to treat a robot as an animal?
Open question
What does meaningful consent look like for participants who cannot fully evaluate what a robot is?

6. Reporting checklist

Adapted from the four groups in Riek's (2012) guidelines, and useful for any HRI study.

Before you submit
  • Robot: which platform, which behaviors, what it can and cannot do, what you expected it to do.
  • Participants: number, age, culture, experience with robots, how recruited, what they were told.
  • Operator: any hidden control, training and error handling.
  • General: setting, scenario, ethics approval, limitations.
  • Measures: instrument, language, reliability, analysis, sample size reasoning.
