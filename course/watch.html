Video 1 of 6 · Social navigation

Meet Jackrabbot: The social robot

Stanford University School of Engineering · 2016

Watch on YouTube

A knee-high, ball-shaped robot learns how people share sidewalks and hallways. It has no face and does not speak. It is social because its movement has to respect personal space, right of way and the unwritten conventions people follow in crowds.

Watch for

  • Which social conventions are never written down, and how would a robot learn them?
  • What does the robot's non-humanoid shape tell you about what "social" requires?

Returns in

Unit 3 (definition), Unit 6 (space and motion).

Video 2 of 6 · The case for social robots

The Rise of Personal Robots, Cynthia Breazeal

TED · talk given at TEDWomen 2010, about 14 minutes

Watch on YouTube

The founder of MIT's Personal Robots Group explains why she built robots that interact with people instead of with objects. She shows Kismet and Leonardo, argues that people respond to a robot's body language much as they respond to a person's, and describes studies in which robots serve as tools for studying human behavior.

Watch for

  • What is a nonverbal cue, and why is it hard to study in two people but easy to control in a robot?
  • Which claims are findings and which are hopes? Write down one of each.

Returns in

Unit 5 (social responses), Unit 7 (robots as research instruments).

Video 3 of 6 · An expressive robot head

Kismet (MIT A.I. Lab)

MIT Artificial Intelligence Laboratory footage

Watch on YouTube

Kismet is a robot head built to engage people through facial expression, vocal tone and attention, not through language. MIT's own project pages describe it generating expressions from a continuous space of arousal, valence and stance, and taking part in turn-taking exchanges with babble instead of words.

Watch for

  • How does the face show that the robot is interested, overwhelmed or sad?
  • Could you read each expression correctly without being told what it was?

Returns in

Unit 6 (expression, believability and readability).

Video 4 of 6 · The central psychological puzzle

Uncanny Valley Revisited: Masahiro Mori

IEEE Spectrum · Mori's own talk

Watch on YouTube

The roboticist who proposed the uncanny valley in 1970 explains where the idea came from: his work on prosthetic hands. He plots affinity against human likeness, shows where industrial robots, toy robots, prosthetic hands and Bunraku puppets fall, and says movement amplifies the effect. He ends by recommending a deliberately nonhuman design and by listing the scientific questions that remain open.

Watch for

  • Where does he place the Bunraku puppet, and why does distance from the stage matter?
  • What does he say is still unknown?

Returns in

Unit 5 (uncanny valley), Unit 4 (choosing a form).

Video 5 of 6 · Education

Tega, the new robot in school

MIT Spectrum · Personal Robots Group

Watch on YouTube

A research scientist describes Tega, a robot used in preschool language studies. It trades stories with a child, asks questions about them, and uses the child's answers to judge language ability and engagement. MIT's project page presents Tega as a platform for long-term, in-home interaction with young children.

Watch for

  • What does the robot measure about the child, and what does it do with that information?
  • Why does the researcher say the robot's behaviors must be designed carefully?

Returns in

Unit 4 (education), Unit 6 (adaptation), Unit 7 (child participants).

Video 6 of 6 · Care

PARO therapeutic robot

Passages Hospice · 2011

Watch on YouTube

A hospice program uses PARO, a robot modeled on a baby seal, with people who have dementia. It responds to touch and to its name. The program argues that patients who struggle to communicate with people may respond more readily to an animal.

Watch for

  • Who benefits here: the patient, the caregiver, or both?
  • Is it deceptive for a person with dementia to treat the robot as an animal? Keep the question for Unit 7.

Returns in

Unit 4 (care), Unit 7 (ethics).
