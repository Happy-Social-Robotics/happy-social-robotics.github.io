Unit 4 of 8

Types of social robots

Sort a robot three ways: by form, by application, by role.

The story

Put five robots on one bench: a head that pulls faces, a seal you can stroke, a tutor for preschoolers, a ball that dodges pedestrians and a humanoid that gestures. Who would you hand each one to, and why?

1. By form

Anthropomorphic

Resembles a human body or face.

ExampleNexi, shown in Breazeal’s talk

Zoomorphic

Resembles an animal.

ExamplePARO, a baby harp seal

Caricatured

Exaggerated, simplified, creature-like features.

ExampleKismet; Tega

Functional

Shaped by its task. Social through behavior.

ExampleJackrabbot, a ball on wheels

Four categories from Fong et al.; the examples are my own teaching classification.

ClaimForm sets expectations. The more humanlike the robot, the more social skill people expect.
Evidence

A very humanlike appearance can create expectations the robot cannot meet. Mori recommends a deliberately nonhuman design to keep affinity safe.

Bartneck et al. 2009Mori 2012
Implication

Match appearance to ability. A face that promises conversation should deliver it.

Open question

How do expectations shift as people see more robots?

2. By application

0.70Mean effect size, cognitive outcomes (education)
0.59Mean effect size, affective outcomes
17–24Average participants per data point

Education

Tutors or peer learners. Came close to human tutoring on restricted tasks.

Health and elder care

Comfort and communication, especially with dementia. Ethical questions are sharpest here.

Child development

Language, storytelling, social-emotional skills. Tega was built for long-term home use. Extra ethics care.

Public spaces

Social navigation: personal space, yielding, sharing paths without explicit rules.

Research instruments

A robot repeats a cue exactly, the same way, for every participant.

Effect sizes: Belpaeme et al. (2018), described as medium. The small samples are a gap a beginner can use.
