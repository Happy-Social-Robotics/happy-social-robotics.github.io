Types of social robots

There is no single taxonomy. Researchers sort robots three ways at once: by form, by application and by the role the robot plays for the person. A paper usually needs all three.

1. By form

Fong et al. group robot embodiment into four broad categories. The examples below are my own classification for teaching, so another reader may place a robot differently.

FormIdeaExample from this primer
AnthropomorphicResembles a human body or faceHumanoid robots such as Nexi, shown in Breazeal's talk
ZoomorphicResembles an animalPARO, modeled on a baby harp seal
CaricaturedExaggerated, simplified, creature-like features that signal social abilityKismet; Tega with its expressive face
FunctionalShaped by its task, social through behaviorJackrabbot, a ball on wheels
Claim
Form sets expectations. The more humanlike the robot looks, the more social skill people expect from it.
Evidence
Bartneck et al. (2009) warn that a very humanlike appearance can create expectations the robot cannot meet. A robot with a human face leads a new user to expect that it can listen and talk. Mori (1970/2012) goes further and recommends a deliberately nonhuman design to keep affinity safe.
Implication
Match appearance to ability. A face that promises conversation should deliver it.
Open question
How do expectations shift as people see more robots? Bartneck et al. note that perceptions are not stable as people grow used to robots.

2. By application

Education

Robots act as tutors or peer learners. Belpaeme et al. (2018) pooled studies in which a robot was compared with an alternative such as a screen tutor or a human tutor. The mean effect size was about 0.70 for cognitive outcomes and 0.59 for affective outcomes, which they describe as medium. Robots came close to human tutoring on restricted tasks. The same review shows a weakness a beginner can use: the average study had only about 17 to 24 participants per data point.

Health and elder care

Socially assistive robots such as PARO are used for comfort, engagement and communication, especially with people who have dementia. This is also where the ethical questions are sharpest (Unit 7).

Child development and therapy

Robots for children appear in language learning, storytelling and social-emotional skills. Tega was built for long-term, in-home interaction with young children. Children are a vulnerable population, so ethics and data handling carry extra weight.

Public and shared spaces

Robots that move among people need social navigation. Jackrabbot's research asks how a robot should respect personal space, yield and share paths without explicit rules.

Research instruments

Breazeal argues that a robot can be a precise scientific tool, because it can reproduce a nonverbal cue exactly, the same way, for every participant. Many HRI studies use a robot this way and are really psychology studies with a robot as the stimulus.

3. By role

  • Tutor or peer learner. Teaches, or learns alongside, the user.
  • Companion or comforter. Provides presence and emotional support.
  • Coach. Supports a goal such as exercise or a diet, as in Breazeal's examples.
  • Co-pedestrian or co-worker. Shares space or a task without being the focus of attention.
  • Stimulus or confederate. A controlled partner in an experiment.

For your first paper

Pick one population, one setting and one behavior. "Social robots for children" is a field. "Does a robot's gaze toward the book change how much preschoolers recall from a story?" is a study.
