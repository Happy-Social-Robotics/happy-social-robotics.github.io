Unit 3 of 8

Foundations: what makes a robot social?

The videos showed a ball on wheels, an expressive head, a classroom companion and a seal. This unit gives you one definition that covers all of them, a short history, and a simple model of how any social robot works.

1. A working definition

Claim
A robot is social when social interaction with people is important to what it is for. Appearance is not the test.
Evidence
Fong, Nourbakhsh and Dautenhahn (2003) review "socially interactive robots", meaning robots for which social human-robot interaction is important, and build a taxonomy of design methods and system components around that idea.
Implication
Judge a design by the interaction it must support. A robot that must yield in a corridor and a robot that must comfort a patient have different social requirements, and neither needs to look human.
Open question
Where is the boundary? A voice assistant with no body, a telepresence robot and a robot vacuum that people name and talk to all sit near the line. Many papers still begin by arguing where their robot falls.

Two traditions called "social"

Fong et al. trace two lines of work. In the first, many simple robots coordinate through collective behavior inspired by social insects. Such societies are anonymous: any member can stand in for another. In the second, individuals matter. Partners recognize each other, form relationships and follow shared norms. Dautenhahn and Billard describe social robots in this second sense as embodied agents within a mixed group of robots and humans that recognize one another, interact socially, have histories and communicate and learn from each other. This course follows the second tradition.

Five questions to place any robot
  1. Who interacts with it?
  2. In what setting, and for how long?
  3. What must it understand about people?
  4. What does it express back, and through which channel?
  5. What changes for the person afterward?

Try the questions on Jackrabbot, Kismet, Tega and PARO. Your answers will differ on almost every line. That variety is why the field needs several kinds of expertise.

3. A simple loop

Most social robots can be understood as a repeating loop. This is a teaching simplification, not Fong et al.'s taxonomy, but it helps you ask where a given study intervenes.

  1. SenseCameras, microphones, touch, distance sensors.
  2. InterpretFaces, speech, gestures, who is where, what they want.
  3. DecideChoose a response given goals, norms and the person's state.
  4. ExpressGaze, face, voice, gesture, movement.
  5. LearnUpdate a model of this person or situation over time.

Kismet is best known for Express. Jackrabbot's research centers on Sense and Decide for movement. Tega's storytelling adds Learn: it updates its belief about the child's engagement. A study can focus on one step, or compare two versions of the same step with people.

4. Why a body?

Claim
Physical presence can improve interaction, but it has to earn its cost.
Evidence
Belpaeme et al. (2018) credit much of the success of robot tutors to physical presence that traditional learning technology lacks. They also note that virtual agents can offer some of the same capabilities without the expense, upkeep and installation burden, so using a robot should be explicitly justified.
Implication
In your own work, say what the body adds: touch, shared physical space, gaze toward objects, attention from other people in the room. If the answer is "nothing", test a screen agent instead.
Open question
Which effects come from embodiment itself and which from novelty? Long-term studies are still rare.

5. Neighboring fields

The HRI conference describes its community as spanning robotics, human-computer interaction, human factors, artificial intelligence, engineering and the social and behavioral sciences. Expect to read outside your home discipline. If you come from engineering, spend time on psychology (Unit 5) and methods (Unit 7). If you come from psychology, learn what the hardware can and cannot do.
