Unit 5 of 8

The psychology of meeting a robot

Six ideas explain most of what you will read in HRI papers.

The story

In experiments, people who knew they were using a machine still showed it politeness. Nobody asked them to. This unit is about why, and what it means for anyone who builds a robot.

1. People respond socially to machines

ClaimPeople apply everyday social rules to machines without deciding to, and without believing the machine is human.
Evidence

Experiments show gender stereotypes, politeness and reciprocity toward computers. Deliberate anthropomorphism did not explain it, so the authors call it mindless.

Reeves & Nass 1996Nass & Moon 2000
Implication

A robot need not be convincing to be treated socially. Every cue you add or remove is an experimental decision.

Open question

Most work used screens. What holds when the machine shares the room, moves and touches?

2. Anthropomorphism

ClaimPeople vary in how much human they see in nonhuman agents. Three sources explain it.
Evidence

Knowledge about humans that is easy to apply; the wish to predict the agent; the wish for social connection. Loneliness predicts more anthropomorphism.

Epley et al. 2007
Implication

Participant state matters, not only robot design. Measure or control loneliness, goals and prior knowledge.

Open question

Do the three factors hold over weeks, or wear off with familiarity?

  • Elicited agent knowledge
  • Effectance motivation
  • Sociality motivation

3. The uncanny valley

Schematic after Mori (1970). Placement is approximate, not data.

ClaimAffinity rises with human likeness, drops sharply near human, then recovers at full human likeness.
Evidence

Proposed by Mori in a 1970 essay; translated into English in 2012. He called it a hypothesis, said movement amplifies it, and left the cause open.

Mori 2012
Implication

Cite it as a hypothesis, not a law. If your robot is near-human, test for the dip instead of assuming it.

Open question

What is the mechanism, and does it hold for movement, voice and behavior, not just faces?

4. Mind perception

Agency: can act, plan, self-controlExperience: can feel hunger, fear →A robot can seem to act without seeming to feel
ClaimPeople judge “mind” on two separate dimensions: Experience and Agency.
Evidence

Participants compared the mental capacities of human and nonhuman characters. Factor analysis gave Experience (feeling) and Agency (self-control). They predicted different moral judgments.

Gray et al. 2007
Implication

“Intelligent” and “has feelings” measure different things. Ask the one you mean.

Open question

Which design choices move agency and experience separately, and what does that do to blame, care and trust?

5. Trust

Robot performanceLargest association
AppearanceWeak
Human factorsLittle evidence
ClaimTrust follows what the robot does more than how it looks or who the user is.
Evidence

Robot performance, such as reliability and failure rate, had the largest association. Appearance was weak. Human factors showed little evidence. The evidence base was small.

Hancock et al. 2011
Implication

Check the robot works reliably before testing personality or appearance. A failure can swamp your effect.

Open question

How is trust repaired after an error, and does it differ by role?

6. People differ

ClaimCulture, prior experience, personality and age all shape how a robot is perceived.
Evidence

Cultural background, experience with robots and personality influence perception. Validated translations of questionnaires are needed. RO-MAN 2027 has a cross-cultural theme.

Bartneck et al. 2009
Implication

Report your sample: age, culture, experience with robots. Do not generalize beyond it.

Open question

Most samples are small and from a few countries. Replication elsewhere is a realistic beginner project.

Read further afieldLabs studying how people respond to avatars and virtual humans ask similar questions. Stanford’s Virtual Human Interaction Lab publishes openly, and findings often transfer to robots.
