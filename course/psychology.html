Unit 5 of 8

The psychology of meeting a robot

Social robotics works because of how people think, not because of what robots are. Six ideas explain most of what you will read in HRI papers.

1. People respond socially to machines

Claim
People apply everyday social rules to computers and other machines without deciding to, and without believing the machine is human.
Evidence
Reeves and Nass (1996) gathered experiments under the label the media equation. Nass and Moon (2000) review studies in which people applied gender stereotypes to computers, showed politeness and reciprocity toward them, and responded to computer "personality". Alternative explanations such as deliberate anthropomorphism did not account for the results, which led the authors to describe the behavior as mindless.
Implication
A robot does not have to be convincing to be treated socially. A small social cue such as gaze toward the user can change behavior, so every cue you add or remove is an experimental decision.
Open question
Most of this work used screens. Which results hold when the machine shares the room, moves and touches? That is the gap robots fill.

2. Anthropomorphism

Claim
People vary in how much they see human traits in nonhuman agents. The variation has three psychological sources.
Evidence
Epley, Waytz and Cacioppo (2007) name them: how accessible and applicable human-based knowledge is (elicited agent knowledge), the motivation to understand and predict an agent (effectance motivation), and the desire for social connection (sociality motivation). The theory predicts more anthropomorphism when people lack social connection. It applies to robotics and also to the reverse process, dehumanization.
Implication
Participant state matters, not only robot design. Loneliness, goals and prior knowledge can all shift ratings of the same robot. Measure or control them.
Open question
Do the three factors predict responses to physical robots over weeks, or do they wear off with familiarity?

3. The uncanny valley

Claim
Affinity for a robot rises as it looks more human, drops sharply when it is almost but not quite human, and recovers at full human likeness.
Evidence
Mori proposed it in a 1970 essay in the Japanese magazine Energy. An authorized English translation by MacDorman and Kageki appeared in 2012. Mori treated it as a hypothesis, said movement amplifies it, and listed the reason people feel this way as an open scientific question.
Implication
Cite it as a hypothesis with a design motive, not as an established law. If your robot is near-human, test for it instead of assuming it. If it is clearly not human, you can still discuss expectations (Unit 4).
Open question
What is the underlying mechanism, and does it hold for movement, voice and behavior as well as for faces?

4. Mind perception

Claim
When people judge how much "mind" something has, they use two separate dimensions.
Evidence
Gray, Gray and Wegner (2007) asked participants to compare the mental capacities of human and nonhuman characters. Factor analysis gave Experience, such as the capacity to feel hunger, and Agency, such as self-control. The two predicted different moral judgments.
Implication
A robot can seem capable of acting without seeming capable of feeling. When you ask whether a robot is "intelligent" or "has feelings", you are measuring different things.
Open question
Which design choices move perceived agency and perceived experience separately, and what does that do to blame, care and trust?

5. Trust

Claim
Trust in a robot follows what the robot does more than what it looks like or who the user is.
Evidence
Hancock et al. (2011) meta-analyzed 29 empirical studies. The overall correlational effect was r = +0.26. Robot performance, such as reliability and failure rate, had the largest association with trust, appearance-type attributes were weak, and human-related factors showed little evidence of an effect. The authors stress that the evidence base was small and could change.
Implication
Before testing personality or appearance, check that the robot works reliably. A failure can swamp the effect you meant to study.
Open question
How is trust repaired after an error, and does it differ by role (tutor, companion, co-worker)? Recent HRI workshops on errors and failures, listed in Unit 8, work on this.

6. People differ

Claim
Culture, prior experience, personality and age all shape how a robot is perceived.
Evidence
Bartneck et al. (2009) list cultural background, prior experience with robots and personality among the factors that influence perception, and call for validated translations of questionnaires. The RO-MAN 2027 conference has adopted the theme "Ethical, Affective, and Transparent HRI Across Cultures".
Implication
Report your sample: age, culture, experience with robots. Do not generalize beyond it.
Open question
Most published samples come from a few countries and are small. Replication in other cultures is a realistic, publishable beginner project.
Read further afield

Labs that study social interaction in virtual and augmented reality ask similar questions about how people respond to avatars and virtual humans. Stanford's Virtual Human Interaction Lab publishes openly on this, and its findings often transfer to robots.
